Slide 01

A Recruiting Strategy for Commissioning Engineers

Good morning, everyone, thank you for making time today. Before I get into the strategy, I want to be upfront about where I'm coming from. While my background isn't rooted directly in commissioning engineering, throughout my various roles, across a range of platforms, I've been responsible (either partially or fully) for finding the right people, putting them in the right seat on the bus, and then developing them once they're there.

I've done this through various leadership roles in the Navy, for years as a Field Service Manager at Videojet Technologies where I built and managed distributed teams, and as a clinic director in multiple healthcare settings. I know that last piece may seem a bit out of place, but it's more relevant than it might seem. The skills behind patient acquisition, prospecting for the right partners, screening for real fit, building relationships before you need them, all transfer directly to talent acquisition. From that lens, this presentation is designed to outline how I'd put those skills to work for Motz.

Slide 02

Why This Requires a Real Strategy

So, why does this actually require a real strategy in the first place? In my most recent roles, I built and ran referral networks and clinical teams: prospecting new partners, screening for fit before a patient ever walked in the door, and building relationships long before I needed them. Talent acquisition works the same way, it's patient acquisition with a different kind of candidate.

Before we go any further, I think it's important to honestly address what I've identified as three things that make commissioning hiring uniquely difficult. First, awareness: most engineering graduates have never heard of commissioning, so we can't rely on inbound interest, we have to build awareness actively. Second, travel: seventy-five percent changes how someone lives day to day, it's not something you check a box on and move past. If we're not honest about it at the first screen, we're not protecting the candidate or the firm. Third, continuity: hiring can't be seasonal, a one-off search every time a role opens puts us behind from the start.

That's the challenge. Here's the framework I'd build to solve it.

Slide 03

One Pipeline, Four Entry Points

This is our care pathway, if I can borrow a term from healthcare: a clear route from a first visit all the way through a full treatment plan. In recruiting terms, it's one pipeline, with four entry points. I'd map this the same way I'd map any career ladder, tier by tier, years of experience, and what we're actually betting on at each stage.

The junior candidate is hired more for trajectory than for immediate output, we're betting on where they're headed. Whereas, at the senior level, that bet has matured: they're running their own client relationships and shaping the tier coming behind them.

The point I'm drawing out here is retention, explicitly. In a competitive market, a job description isn't enough, we need to show a candidate a real arc: milestones that are transparent and merit-based, and the next rung they can actually see from wherever they start. This not only protects Motz from costly turnover, but keeps active field teams motivated, and holds institutional knowledge inside the firm instead of walking out the door.

Once we know who we're hiring for each of these four tiers, the next question is how we actually find them.

Slide 04

How I'd Source Talent at Every Level

Now the operational question: how do we actually find these people? At the early-career level, this looks a lot like the referral-partner prospecting I did in Dallas. It means building faculty relationships a year ahead of graduation instead of simply posting a job listing as opportunities become available. It also means running co-op and internship pipelines through a platform like Handshake, so the relationship survives past a single semester.

For more senior candidates, the approach changes completely. Typically, these individuals aren't just sitting around browsing job boards. If someone is performing well as a Commissioning Engineer or Senior PM, they're usually pretty settled and not actively looking. So my focus moves from broad attraction to precise identification. It means watching for BCxA and BCxP credential signals on LinkedIn. It means showing up at BCxA and ASHRAE events where senior CxEs actually are.

And honestly, the highest-value channel at this level is already in this room. The leadership here has built something over the past decade: a real commissioning practice, and a real reputation across this industry. That network is worth more than any outreach campaign I could build from scratch.

Now, I do feel there is one piece of this sourcing picture that deserves its own conversation: the military pipeline.

Slide 05

The Military Pipeline

This next part is about one of Motz's strongest sourcing advantages already in place, the military pipeline, and it's personal for me too. I'm a Navy veteran myself, I served as a Fire Controlman, and I know firsthand how hard it is to translate what you did in uniform into language a civilian hiring manager understands. Motz has already built something real with SkillBridge and it is my job to build the sourcing engine and the translation layer underneath it so it scales.

Veterans from Navy Nuclear, Seabees, and advanced electrical backgrounds bring exactly what commissioning requires: systems thinking, documentation discipline, a safety-first mindset, the same things the Navy drilled into me. Beyond the placements themselves, this means building direct relationships with transition offices at installations with strong technical MOS populations, so Motz is a known name before someone even starts thinking about separating.

And I'd also want us looking beyond the SkillBridge window itself: organizations like Roll Call Chicagoland connect us with veterans already in the civilian market who are looking for the right technical fit, and our project profile and culture should resonate with that group.

And, finally, the leverage point most firms miss is resume translation. These candidates have strong operational experience but don't always know how to map it onto civilian commissioning language, and bridging that gap early reaches a high-caliber pool other firms overlook entirely.

Once someone's in the pipeline, whatever channel they came through, the next question is: how do we evaluate them objectively?

Slide 06

How I Evaluate Candidates

So how do I actually evaluate candidates once they're in front of us? Now, clearly, I am not the technical expert in the room, you all are. What I do bring is the discipline of competency-based interviewing, a skill I've built over more than two decades of leadership and hiring experience, and I used it to build this framework directly off what Motz already uses: safety, credentials, technical depth, and the tools that round it out.

The chart on the right reflects the same idea I raised with the framework earlier: what we're betting on changes by level. A junior candidate isn't expected to walk in with a BCxP. The bet there is coachability, not credentials. While a senior candidate is expected to already have that covered, and what we're really betting on by then is whether they develop the people underneath them. Travel follows the same pattern: early on, it's about willingness. Later, it's about whether someone's actually led a team through it before.

What this demonstrates is that the list itself stays the same at every level. What moves is how much of it we expect someone to already have walking in the door. Which raises the real question: once someone clears that bar, what actually makes them stand out from everyone else who cleared it too? That's exactly what I want to get into next.

Slide 07

Beyond the Resume: What Tips the Scale

This next part is about what actually tips the scale between two equally qualified candidates, the things that may not explicitly show up on a resume. Demonstrated motivation matters most. You can usually tell within the first few minutes whether someone's actually thought about why commissioning, why now, why Motz, or whether they're giving you the answer they think you want to hear.

Honest travel alignment comes next. I don't ask "can you travel seventy-five percent." I ask them to walk me through an actual week, what a Monday looks like, what happens with their kids or their partner or their dog. People who've genuinely thought it through can answer that in detail.

Customer-service instinct is one I actually know a lot about. The way someone describes a disagreement with a contractor or an owner tells you whether they see the client as an obstacle or a partner, and that distinction shows up in the story, not in whether they say the right words. Relevant military or field experience carries real weight here too, for the same reasons I mentioned a few slides back.

Leadership potential shows up even with a junior candidate, in whether they ask me a sharp question back or just wait for the next one. Long-term growth signal is the one people can't fake. Anyone can say they want a career here. What's harder to fake is bringing their own questions about where this could go instead of waiting for me to describe it.

Ideally, I'd like to develop this into an actual scoring rubric so these six things are something the whole hiring panel is weighing the same way. This ensures decision-making is objectively defined rather than left solely to gut feel. This baseline, along with any additional qualities, skills, or certifications the team deems pertinent, gives us the signals to distinguish between comparably qualified candidates.

Next, I'd like to talk about what Motz is actually offering the candidates we choose to hire.

Slide 08

Transformational, Not Transactional

I want to start by telling you where the title of this slide originates, because it isn't originally mine. It comes from a former coworker of mine, Larry Mitchell, who actually recruited me to work at Airrosti. He's spent thirty years in this industry, and today he's a VP of Talent Acquisition. When I reached out to him for advice while I was pursuing this role, he gave me a long list of things to think about. But one phrase stuck with me more than anything else. He said: be transformational, not transactional.

It stuck because it's something I've actually seen play out. Not merely as a concept, but through people I know. Watching their experience is what convinced me this isn't just a phrase. It's something Motz is already doing. Honestly, I think it's one of the things that sets Motz apart from its competition. That phrase, "transformational, not transactional," is the litmus test everything I tell a candidate about Motz has to pass.

Motz isn't selling a job, it's offering a career with genuine depth and trajectory. The conversation looks different depending on who's across the table, but the principle underneath it never changes. For a new graduate, the conversation is about trajectory: a defined path, visible mentorship, a clear picture of where they can be in five years. The opportunity isn't just a job, it's who they can become here.

For an experienced CxE with options, trajectory alone isn't the hook. What they want is proof that the work is substantive and the relationship lasts. That's why we lead with the project portfolio and ninety-one years of operating history. Same firm. Two different conversations. One shared principle.

Now let's talk about the practical side: how long each of these hires actually takes.

Slide 09

Hiring Timeline & Pipeline Cadence

This chart represents a rough estimate of how long I'd expect each of these hires to actually take once the pipeline's up and running. For junior and entry-level roles, I'd estimate four to six weeks. The timeline is only that fast because of the sourcing groundwork we've already talked about: the university relationships, co-op students, and SkillBridge pipeline. Without that groundwork, this would take a lot longer. So the goal is that by the time a role actually opens, we've already been talking to people.

For a mid-level Commissioning Engineer, that timeline stretches out to six to eight weeks. It's less predictable at this level, some of it is warm relationships we've already built, but some of it is genuinely active outreach happening in real time, reaching people who weren't necessarily already in the pipeline.

And for a Senior CxE or PM, I'd plan on eight to twelve weeks, sometimes longer. I definitely wouldn't want to rush that. These are relationship-driven hires, and trying to force one into a shorter timeline is usually how you end up with the wrong person in an important seat.

The thing I want to be clear about is that none of this starts from zero if I'm doing my job right. The pipeline's designed to be running all the time. So these numbers are more representative of how long it takes to close out a role. Building this out is one thing. Knowing whether it's actually working is a different question, and that's the final thing I'd like to go over.

Slide 10

How I'd Measure Success

None of what we've walked through matters if the people coming through this pipeline aren't good hires. A tight pipeline means nothing if quality suffers on the other end. So here are the metrics I'd use to measure whether this is actually working.

On time-to-fill, I'd want trend lines, not one number. One senior search that takes eighteen weeks because the skill set was rare shouldn't make the system look broken. I've built dashboards like this before, and the mistake I've seen most is reacting to one bad month instead of the pattern.

Quality of hire is what I'd weight most, and the six-month hiring manager check-in is the part I'd watch closest. Retention alone can be misleading, someone can stay a full year and just be a mediocre fit the whole time. Pipeline health is whether we're ready to move when we need to. Same idea as tracking new patient calls before anyone's booked an appointment. Wait until the appointment to start counting, and you're already behind.

Source effectiveness is where the budget conversation gets real. If professional networks convert at twice the rate of career fairs a year from now, that's the signal to shift dollars, not a guess. I want military pipeline growth on its own line for a specific reason: aggregate numbers hide exactly the thing you're proudest of. Fold veteran hires into a general count and that progress disappears into a spreadsheet.

And offer acceptance rate matters because it's the first place we'd see whether the story we're telling candidates is landing, or just sounding good in the room. All of that comes back to a small set of principles I want to close on.

Slide 11

Talent at Every Level, Built on One Foundation

Everything we've covered today comes back to five principles that I've identified as the core values of this role.

One, Attract, rather than chase, build the relationships before we need them. Two, Be Precise, rather than oversold, if a claim doesn't hold up, we don't make it. Three, develop Relational equity, do the slow work before the need arrives. Four, maintain a sense of Genuine curiosity, stay interested in what motivates someone, not just what they can do on day one. Five, and Transformational, not transactional, at every level, from the intern walking in the door to the senior PM we're inviting into a long-term career.
